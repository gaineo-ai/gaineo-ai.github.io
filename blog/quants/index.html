We are not your stockbroker. We are your quants.

Systems of record tell you where you stand. A system of optimization tells you where you could stand, and the odds of getting there. Why every company deserves its own quantitative intelligence.

By Aditya Vadrevu
ShareLinkedInEmail

For decades, companies have invested in systems built to answer one question: what happened? We built systems to record transactions, track opportunities, manage customer relationships, reconcile financials and report performance. They brought order to complexity and gave every business a reliable history. They do matter.

Somewhere along the way, decision-making settled into a relay. Information passed through as-of reports, and decisions rested on intuition built from experience. Both looked at the business in the rearview mirror. That relay was the best we could do. With AI, it no longer has to be. The patterns, probabilities and trade-offs that people once pieced together from reports can now be modeled directly, and put in front of the people who make the call.

The stockbroker and the quant

In financial markets, a stockbroker executes trades, maintains positions and reports the value of a portfolio. These are essential functions. But the most sophisticated investment firms do not build their competitive advantage on recording trades. They employ quants, who ask what a portfolio could be worth under different conditions, with different decisions, within acceptable constraints. They model probabilities, test outcomes and optimize continuously.

A system of record tells you where you stand. A system of optimization tells you where you could stand, and the odds of getting there.

Revenue lifecycle’s optimization deficit

Inside a modern company, the CRM knows every opportunity, the CPQ every quote and discount, the ERP every order and invoice. Yet the critical decisions in a forecast call are never about looking backward. Which mix of pricing, discounting and deal priority gives the best odds of reaching this quarter’s target, given the shape of the pipeline and known execution constraints? Where and how can margin be defended without costing the win? How should scarce sales capacity be spent across prospects, products and regions? What is the untapped opportunity in white spaces? What is the cost versus benefit of relaxing longstanding policy or terms?

These questions require connecting patterns and building an enduring systemic understanding, which is currently beyond each of these systems. They are advanced search and optimization problems. Another dashboard, a workflow or even another agent layered onto a system of record cannot solve them.

An eloquent answer is not an optimal decision

Generative AI has made information more accessible than ever, and that is a real advance. An assistant can tell a sales leader that pipeline coverage is short. A quantitative approach goes further: it estimates the odds of reaching the target, sizes the gap, weighs the alternatives against bookings or margin gap, and recommends the combination most likely to land the plan. When conditions change, it recalculates.

Not just predictions, but guidance for better decisions, made systematically, under uncertainty, with people ultimately making the call.

Every company deserves its own quants

For the best investment firms, quants are not a cost center. They are the moat: a model of the market and how they exploit it optimally that competitors cannot copy, and that sharpens with every decision.
